So, the core problem with clarity is usually not I need a better vocabulary, or I'm a rambler, or another one that I hear is my brain goes too fast, or I have ADHD, or I need better preparation. Those are usually not the problem. The problem is very simple. Your brain is processing more information than your words can carry. And you don't have a process for handling that mental congestion. I want to emphasize this concept of mental congestion. Imagine pouring a massive amount of water through a very, very tiny opening.

The water builds up. There's pressure that's increasing, and suddenly you a bottleneck. And that exact phenomenon is what's happening when you speak. A founder gets asked a very important question like, "Why did revenue drop this month?" And inside their head exists the sales numbers, the new hire that just was brought on, the ad campaigns, the seasonality, pipeline quality, customer issues, something marketing did 3 months ago, and the solution that they're considering at the moment. Like, their mind is flooded with ideas, memories, opinions, and possible ways to explain something, but your mouth can only release one word at a time. You just can't keep up.

And now add the pressure of wanting to sound intelligent and probably trying to impress the people that are listening to you, or at least sound like a leader, and choose the perfect word. And the result is you start overthinking. You start second-guessing and start struggling to articulate thoughts that you actually understand. None of these are good. So, we have to learn how to decide what matters, what comes first, what can actually wait, what does this person in front of you need from you? And that's why clear speaking is thought compression.

Compressing what's in our mind into the minimum amount of information that's relevant to our listener. So, the first step is the one that I've seen change people speaking the most, and that's being able to find the point. What we often end up doing when we answer a question is we begin with something like, "Yeah, I think there's a a lot of things that are relevant to this question." Then we start winding through our content like we're lost in a forest. The problem with this is we're discovering our answer in front of everyone. Instead, what I want you to start doing is asking yourself what are called executive action questions, EAQs.

An EAQ is what allows you to filter out what is unnecessary. The most common EAQ is the question, "What is different in the real world after I finish speaking? What is the change? What is the behavioral shift? What is the action that should be taken because of my words?" And so, for high performers, clarity is often connected to an action, something getting done, something moving forward, and you don't need someone to understand the whole process. You need something to happen. Maybe it's you want the team to change the sales process. You want the board to approve this decision. You want this employee to understand exactly what needs to improve. You want the client to trust the strategy. Knowing the outcome often gives you your point.
